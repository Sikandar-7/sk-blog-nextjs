From the outside, food delivery looks like a shopping cart with a map bolted
on. Pick food, pay, wait.

Underneath, it is four different products sharing one database. A customer, a
restaurant, a rider and an operator each look at the same order and need
something completely different from it. Once you see that, most of the design
decisions explain themselves.

## The same order, four ways

**The customer** wants two things: choose food quickly, then stop worrying.
Before ordering they need prices, delivery time and a minimum order value.
After ordering, their only question is "where is it?"

**The restaurant** does not care about your journey. They need incoming orders
in one place, the ability to accept or reject, and a way to say "this is
ready". During a rush, the interface either gets out of the way or it gets
ignored and the shop goes back to phone calls.

**The rider** is on a phone, outdoors, possibly on a bike. They need: what to
pick up, where from, where to, and a way to mark it delivered. Anything else
is noise. They also need to go online and offline, because they are not
available all day.

**The operator** watches the whole marketplace: which restaurants are active,
which riders are online, what has been ordered today, and which orders are
stuck. They also decide who gets to join.

Four audiences, one order record, four separate dashboards.

## Where an order actually comes from

The interesting part of a marketplace is not the cart, it is what the platform
refuses to take on trust.

A cart arrives from the customer's browser. It knows what they picked. It also
knows what *they think* it costs — and that number cannot be believed. Not
because customers are dishonest, but because anything a browser sends can be
edited before it arrives.

So the server rebuilds the order from scratch. It takes the item identifiers,
looks up its own prices, applies its own rules, and calculates its own total.
The client says what it wants. The server decides what it costs.

The same principle covers everything money touches:

- **Minimum order value** belongs to the restaurant, not the cart
- **Delivery fee** depends on the restaurant and whether it is delivery or
  collection
- **Discounts** come from the coupon record, with its own limits
- **Extras** — sizes, toppings — are priced from the menu, not from the request

None of this is visible when things go well. All of it matters the first time
somebody tries.

## Status is the spine of the product

An order is not a row that gets created and forgotten. It is a small state
machine that four different people push forward:

```
pending → accepted → preparing → ready → on the way → delivered
```

Every panel is really a view onto this. The restaurant moves it from pending
to ready. The rider takes it from ready to delivered. The customer watches it
move. The admin steps in when it stops moving.

Two things I would get right from the start next time. First, **not everyone
may make every transition** — a restaurant should not be able to mark someone
else's order delivered, and that has to be enforced where the change happens,
not in the UI that offers the button. Second, **some transitions have side
effects.** Marking an order delivered is also a statement about payment for a
cash order. Anything that quietly changes money deserves more scrutiny than a
status dropdown suggests.

## What surprised me

**Restaurant hours are a real feature.** A closed restaurant taking orders is
worse than a restaurant that is not listed at all, because a customer waits
for food that nobody is cooking.

**Availability is per item, not per restaurant.** Kitchens run out of one
thing constantly. If the menu cannot express that, staff start rejecting
orders manually, and the rejection is a bad customer experience that the
system caused.

**Riders need less than you think.** Every extra element on that screen is one
more thing to tap while holding a helmet. The rider panel got simpler with
every revision, and it improved each time.

**The admin panel is the product for the person running the business.** It is
easy to spend all the design effort on the customer app and leave the operator
with a table of raw data. They are the one using it all day.

## The honest summary

The hard parts of a delivery platform are not technical novelties. They are:

- deciding what the server refuses to trust
- making sure the right person can make each change
- keeping four interfaces consistent about one shared reality

Get those right and the rest is ordinary product work. Get them wrong and you
find out from a customer, which is a considerably more expensive way to learn.
